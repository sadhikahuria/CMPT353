1. Yeah it does feel a bit like p-hacking. I ran 4 tests total and the first two (for all users) were not significant at all, with p values of 0.168 and 0.141. then looking at just instructors gave p values of 0.052 and 0.045, which are way closer to significance. The problem is that decision to look at instructors separately came after seeing that the overall results were not significant, so it feels pretty post-hoc.
Also, when you run 4 tests at p < 0.05, the chance of getting at least one false positive just by chance is (1 - 0.95^4) which is about 18.5%. and if we apply a bonferroni correction (threshold = 0.05/4 = 0.0125), neither of the instructor results would even be significant. So, it seems reaosnable for looking at instructors separately since they actually use the search feature, but I don't think I woould feel super comfortable drawing a strong conclusion at p < 0.05 here.

2. With 7 implementations, the number of pairwise T-tests would be C(7, 2) = 21 tests. If we looked for p < 0.05 across all 21 of them, the probability of getting at least one false conclusion just by chance would be: 1 - 0.95^21 = about 0.659
So roughly a 66% chance of a false positive somewhere, which is way too high to trust. That is why we would use ANOVA first to check if there is any difference at all, and then Tukey HSD as the post-hoc test since it already accounts for doing multiple comparisons. If I had used Bonferroni instead, the corrected threshold would have been 0.05 / 21 which is about 0.0024.

3. Based on the ANOVA (which confirmed there are significant differences) and the Tukey HSD pairwise comparisons, the ranking from fastest to slowest is partition_sort, then qs1, then qs5, then qs2 and qs3 together, then merge1, and then finally qs4 as the slowest.
The only pair that could NOT be distinguished from each other was qs2 and qs3, with a Tukey HSD p value of 0.94. Every other pair was statistically distinguishable with p values basically at 0.
